## Verdict

The argument holds. The structure — chain / tracks / constraints, then gaps, then what a profile
does and does not fix, then options — is the right shape for this audience, and the discipline of
making "no" appear as often as it honestly should is what will make it credible in Brussels.
Nothing below asks you to reopen a structural decision.

What has gone stale is narrower than "older information" suggests, but it is concentrated in the
places where the report is most specific, which is where a reviewer will look:

1. **The protocol-profiling caution has been overtaken.** Three of the five TLS rows moved
   between July and September 2026. Two are now published RFCs and two are in the RFC Editor
   queue. The report's "still in motion" caution — which appears in the executive summary, §3.2,
   §3.5, §7.1, §11.8 and §12.3 — needs re-basing, not deleting.
2. **The hardware/certification evidence has been overtaken.** Two HSMs now hold FIPS 140-3
   Level 3 validations covering PQC.
3. **A US instrument has appeared that changes the urgency of the central recommendation.**
   EO 14412 directs CISA and NIST to publish *minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of
   materials* by roughly March 2027. The report cites the EO once, in Annex D, and never uses
   it. This is the single largest missed opportunity in the draft.
4. **Two live EU legislative files are absent entirely** — CSA2 and the EUCC/certification
   capacity picture — and both bear directly on §11.7 custodianship and the LYR-T-03 argument.
5. **The first NIS CG roadmap milestone falls in about three months** and the report does not
   exploit it.

Plus a set of internal-consistency errors (§C) and a publication-readiness gap (§D) that is
larger than the factual work: the draft still reads as a working document, and Annex B — which
recommendation RDY-02 depends on — is an empty stub.

Priority worklist is at the end.

### A2. Hardware and certification — §3.3 (LYR-T-02, LYR-T-03), §7.5, §10.5 R3

The claim "as of early 2026 no HSM vendor had completed a Level 3 validation covering

### A3. SPDX maturity — §7.1 (GAP-STD-01), §3.4 (LYR-X-02 anecdote)

"The other's cryptography workstream took its first structural decisions in 2026 and describes
itself as at the beginning" understates where SPDX now is. As of the spring 2026 update: six
`CryptoClass` values (including MAC, KDF and RNG), inclusion/removal criteria for algorithms,
cardinality on property definitions, parameter representation in progress starting with block
cipher modes, and **PQC as a new orthogonal property** under development. First external
user-contributor (SCANOSS) consuming the algorithm data.

Source: [SPDX Cryptographic Algorithm List: Spring 2026 Update](https://toscalix.com/2026/05/28/spdx-cryptographic-algorithm-list-spring-2026-update/)

The **asymmetry claim survives** — one format has a ratified cryptographic object model, the other
is building an algorithm list — and that is all GAP-STD-01 needs. Reword from "at the beginning"
to a stage description, because the current wording will be read as out of date by anyone tracking
the workstream, and it makes the report look like it is arguing for a format.

The same source is the primary for your LYR-X-02 anecdote ("it lacked a single contributor with
the knowledge and the time to do it"). Worth citing it directly rather than as "one of the two
major BOM standards" — the anonymisation buys nothing and the quote is public. Note also the
coordinator role is externally sponsored, which is a second, sharper capacity datum.

Add the format-share figures from the ENISA survey (below) here: **CycloneDX 44%, SPDX 29%,
proprietary 17%, non-standard 11%**. They are the empirical basis for "any instrument naming a
single format picks a winner" — 29% of the market is on the other one — and for RDY-02.

The report paraphrases the survey correctly but never states its figures or its size, and chapter

Source: [ENISA, *SBOM Adoption State of Play – 2026*](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/publications/sbom-adoption-state-of-play-2026) ([PDF](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-06/SBOM%20Adoption%20State%20of%20Play%202026.pdf))

- attribute the 70% explicitly to the separate study, in the same sentence, and say why the two

The first is stronger and costs two clauses. Leaving it as is, is the only place in the report

**Also state n = 334 and that the sample is self-selected.** A 334-respondent voluntary survey
carrying a chapter of a Commission report is fine if declared and a liability if not. One
sentence: what it is, what it is not, and why the direction of the findings is robust even if the
magnitudes are not.

## B. Substantive additions

These are not corrections. Each changes what the report argues or how urgent it is.

### B1. EO 14412 — the US is about to specify CBOM minimum elements

**This is the most important addition.** Executive Order 14412, *Securing the Nation Against
Advanced Cryptographic Attacks*, signed **22 June 2026**:

- **CISA and NIST must release guidance within 270 days** — approximately **19 March 2027** —
  "describing minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of materials", explicitly to enable
  **automated assessment** of cryptographic assets in hardware or software.
- Agencies must review HVA and high-impact system inventories within 90 days and produce
  migration plans.
- PQC for key establishment on all HVAs and high-impact systems by **31 December 2030**; for
  digital signatures by **31 December 2031**.
- The **FAR Council** must propose rules requiring covered contractors to comply with NIST
  standards by 31 December 2030; contractors must incorporate cryptographic vulnerability reports
  into vulnerability disclosure policies within 270 days.
- NIST pilot project completion by 31 December 2027.

Sources: [Federal Register, 25 June 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-25/pdf/2026-12909.pdf);
[full text](https://eo.governmentreporter.ai/orders/2026-12909/full-text)

The report currently carries EO 14412 as a single unannotated line in Annex D.4b. It should be
load-bearing in four places:

1. **§5.4 (or a new §5.5), "the space is being filled".** The report's existing argument is that
   in the absence of a Union answer, national answers arrive anyway — evidenced by BSI. EO 14412
   makes that argument transatlantic and dated. A US federal specification of CBOM minimum
   elements, aimed at automated assessment and tied to federal procurement, lands roughly fifteen
   months before the CRA applies in full. If no EU baseline exists by then, the de facto reference
   for European suppliers selling into both markets is American, and the EU exercise becomes
   reconciliation rather than specification.
2. **Chapter 6's own critique applies to it.** LSN-02 is that minimum-element floors are set too
   low to act on — the specific lesson of the SBOM minimum-elements approach, which you have
   already analysed in `analysis-2026-sbom-minimum-elements.md`. The US is about to apply exactly
   that pattern to cryptography. That is not a rhetorical point against the US; it is the report's
   central thesis (define the profile against a decision, not a floor against an obligation)
   receiving a live test case. Use it.
3. **§12.3 sequencing.** The report's "Now" bucket has no external deadline. It has one:
   RDY-01 and RDY-09 are materially cheaper before March 2027 than after. A European
   profile-shaped contribution to the US minimum-elements process, or at minimum a published
   baseline that the US work can map to, is a different and much better position than arriving
   afterwards.
4. **Executive summary.** One sentence.

Note also the shape of the convergence: the EO's stated purpose for CBOM minimum elements is
*automated assessment*. That is §8.1's mechanism, arriving independently in another jurisdiction.
The report should say so — it is external corroboration of the report's core claim, and it costs
two sentences.

### B2. CSA2 — the live vehicle, and what it does to custodianship

The Cybersecurity Act revision, proposed **January 2026** (roughly 271 pages, in the legislative
procedure alongside COM(2026) 13), restructures EU cybersecurity governance around four pillars:
supply-chain harmonisation, a revised certification framework, NIS2 simplification, and reinforced
ENISA authority. It **formally establishes ENISA as scheme manager with dedicated governance
structures**, replacing ad hoc arrangements.

Sources: [Cullen International](https://www.cullen-international.com/news/2026/02/Revised-Cybersecurity-Act--CSA2----Changes-to-the-EU-cybersecurity-certification-framework.html);
[Covington](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/european-commission-proposes-cybersecurity-act-2-new-eu-supply-chain-rules-and-certification-reforms/);
[craevidence.com on the ENISA certification conference](https://craevidence.com/blog/enisa-certification-conference-2026)

CSA2 appears nowhere in the report. It should appear in three places:

- **§11.7 custodianship.** The ENISA option's weakness column currently reads "capacity; change
  cadence slower than the specification churn". CSA2 partially answers the governance half of
  that: a formal scheme-manager role with dedicated structures is a better home for a versioned
  specification than an agency doing it informally. The capacity objection stands. Rewrite the row
  with CSA2 named, and keep DR-01 — you still are not recommending an option.
- **§11 framing.** "Nothing here requires new legislation" remains true and should stay. But two
  relevant files are open right now, and a specification-shaped contribution is cheaper as an
  input to an open file than as an amendment to a closed one. That is a sequencing fact, not a
  call for legislation.
- **§3.3 LYR-T-03.** CSA2 changes the certification framework the track describes.

### B3. EUCC — European evidence for a claim currently carried by FIPS

§3.3 (LYR-T-03), §7.1 (GAP-STD-05) and §11.8 all rest on certification throughput being
structurally limited, evidenced by FIPS/CMVP timelines. For a Commission audience the European
evidence is better and it exists:

- EUCC issued **29 certificates in its first operational year**, with **28 accredited conformity
  assessment bodies** across the EU.
- Europe accounts for more than 60% of roughly 350 global Common Criteria certificates issued
  annually; all EU CC certificates were at CC-1 as of February 2026.
- The Commission set a **December 2026** aspiration for sufficient notified bodies to be
  operational — policy intent, not guaranteed per Member State.
- **Two-thirds of European countries do not yet have an eIDAS national accreditation body.**
- Module availability diverges by Member State (Germany prioritising Module H, others Module B).
- ENISA is running **18 pilots** on EUCC-to-CRA presumption of conformity, alongside EUDI Wallet
  and Managed Security Services schemes.

Source: [craevidence.com](https://craevidence.com/blog/enisa-certification-conference-2026) — secondary; **[check]** against ENISA and the EUCC
certificate list.

29 certificates in a year, against a CRA that applies to products with digital elements across the
Union, is a stronger statement of the rate limiter than any FIPS figure, and it is the
Commission's own machinery. It also gives §11.8's phasing caution a European number.

The report should also name **EUCC** in LYR-T-03's opening sentence. As written the track lists
"FIPS and CMVP validation, Common Criteria evaluation, and national schemes" — omitting the EU's
own scheme from a report addressed to the Commission is the kind of gap a reviewer notices first.

### B4. Three EU hooks the report does not use

- **DORA.** §11.6 names finance as a sector needing its own profile and offers no instrument.
  DORA is the obvious anchor: ICT risk management and third-party oversight obligations on
  financial entities, with a register of information on ICT third-party arrangements. A
  finance-sector profile composing on the baseline has a supervisory consumer already in place.
- **eIDAS2 / the EU Digital Identity Wallet.** A Union-run, crypto-dependent programme with its
  own certification scheme in development at ENISA, Member State implementations, and a
  well-defined set of cryptographic relationships between wallet, issuer and relying party. It is
  the best available candidate for a **pathfinder consumer** of the baseline profile — better than
  a generic procurement case, because the Commission can direct it. Worth a paragraph in §11.1 or
  §11.6.
- **The Quantum Act** (Commission work programme 2026, Q2 2026) is industrial and supply-chain
  policy for quantum technologies, not cryptographic migration.
  Source: [EP Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-new-plan-for-europe-s-sustainable-prosperity-and-competitiveness/file-quantum-act).
  Mention it in one line precisely to say it is **not** the vehicle — otherwise a reader assumes
  the report overlooked it.

### B5. Three objections the report does not answer

The report is unusually good at stating its own limits. These three are not in §8.6 and a
Commission reader will raise all of them.

**1. The aggregation objection — is a CBOM an attacker's map?** §10.8 excludes key material and
configuration secrets, and GAP-COM-03 treats disclosure resistance as a commercial matter. Neither
addresses the security objection: a structured, machine-readable record of where weak or
classical cryptography sits across European critical infrastructure, aggregated by a supervisory
authority, is itself a high-value target — and the report's own §11.5 recommends exactly that
aggregation. This needs a short subsection, not a sentence. The material for it is mostly already
in the draft: bounded disclosure as a purpose of obligation levels (§9.2), the operational-role
attribute reducing the number of entries that indicate real exposure (R3a), scope statements
limiting what any single artefact reveals, and the distinction between an artefact held in a
procurement relationship and an artefact pooled in a supervisory repository. `design-note-data-and-hndl.md`
in the repository probably carries the rest. Recommend a §10.8a or a new §8.7.

**2. Proportionality and SME burden.** The CRA's political history is largely an argument about
burden on smaller manufacturers, and the report recommends obligations landing at LYR-C-04 with
no burden analysis anywhere. The answer is available and favourable: ENISA's evidence is that
generation is close to solved and largely automated in build pipelines, so the marginal cost of
cryptographic content on an existing SBOM pipeline is low — which is the report's own §6.3
argument, currently used only to establish credibility rather than proportionality. Add a short
passage making the proportionality case explicitly, and state that the baseline's obligation
levels set a **ceiling** as well as a floor. Without this the report is easy to characterise as
another reporting obligation.

**3. Why not simply extend the CRA's SBOM minimum elements?** This is the cheapest alternative to
everything the report recommends, it is what the US is about to do (B1), and the report never
addresses it head-on. Chapter 6 contains the answer — minimum-element floors are set to what
everyone can produce rather than to what a decision requires, which is how you get artefacts
produced and not read — but it is never aimed at this specific counter-proposal. One subsection
in chapter 8 or a §12.4 entry. §12.4 is the natural home: "what this report does not recommend"
should include "extending an SBOM minimum-elements floor to cryptography", with the reason.

### B7. HSMs offer PQC while their own protection stays classical

This is the sharpest correction in the review, and it supersedes part of A2.

The two new Level 3 validations do not mean two quantum-safe modules exist. A FIPS 140-3
validation covers the algorithms inside the module boundary. It says nothing about the
cryptography that protects that boundary. A module can offer ML-KEM and ML-DSA to applications
while its own firmware signing, secure boot, administrative authentication, key backup and
wrapping, attestation, and host channel all remain classical. An adversary with a CRQC does not
need to break the PQC algorithms the module offers. It can attack the classical cryptography that
protects the module instead.

The industry distinction is **external** versus **internal** PQC support. External support means
PQC algorithms exposed through the API, usually delivered by firmware update. Internal support
means the module's own architecture — root signing keys, secure boot, internal key wrapping,
component communications — is quantum-safe. Almost all vendors offer the first. Very few have
redesigned for the second. Of the two newly validated modules, Crypto4A is cited as having a
fully integrated quantum-safe design with hybrid roots of trust and PQC-secured boot; the Thales
TCT validation is firmware-delivered algorithm support.

Sources: [ABI Research, *What Does Quantum-Safe Really Mean for HSM Vendors?*](https://www.abiresearch.com/blog/quantum-security-for-hardware-security-modules-hsms);
[draft-reddy-pquip-pqc-hsm](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/draft-reddy-pquip-pqc-hsm-00) (firmware signing only — it recommends
SLH-DSA, HSS-LMS or XMSS for code signing rather than ML-DSA, and does not cover the other
internal functions). Both secondary or draft — **[check]** before citing, and the working-group
observation convention in §7.1 may be the honest label for the wider claim.

**Why this matters more than the factual correction.** It is the best worked demonstration of the
report's own structural claim, and the report does not use it.

A module is not one thing with one cryptographic posture. It is a set of interfaces. Each
interface is a cryptographic relationship with a different party at the far end:

| Interface | Far endpoint | Who owns the migration |
|---|---|---|
| Application cryptographic API | The calling application | Module vendor, then the integrator |
| Firmware update authentication | The vendor's signing infrastructure | Module vendor alone |
| Administrative authentication | Operators, smartcards, quorum tokens | Vendor, then the operator |
| Key backup, wrapping and cloning | Another module, or offline storage | Vendor, then the operator |
| Attestation | A verifier, often a third party | Vendor |
| Secure channel to the host | The host platform | Vendor and integrator |

A product-level "PQC supported" flag collapses all six rows into one bit. That bit is true and
misleading, which is exactly the failure §10.5 R1 already describes for TLS. The algorithm choice
also differs by row — hash-based signatures are preferred for firmware authentication, lattice
signatures for general use — so a single algorithm list cannot describe the module even in
principle.

A relationship-based profile handles this. Each interface gets its own row, its own algorithms,
its own `lifecycleStage` and its own classical retirement state. The buyer can then see that the
application API is post-quantum while firmware authentication is not, and that only the vendor can
fix the second. That is the same split-state finding as §3.3 LYR-T-01, arriving in hardware.

**Recommended edits:**

1. **§3.3 LYR-T-02** — rewrite. State the two validations. Then state plainly that a validation
   covers algorithms inside the boundary and not the cryptography protecting the boundary. Give
   the internal/external distinction. This replaces "no HSM vendor had completed a Level 3
   validation" with something both current and more useful.
2. **§10.5 — new relationship R4.** The worked example currently has three relationships. Add a
   fourth: a hardware module described interface by interface, with the table above as its
   content. This is the report's strongest single illustration of why the unit of description is
   the relationship, and it lands in hardware, where the migration is a capital decision.
   Recommend making it R4 and keeping the existing R3 as the simpler hardware-anchoring case.
3. **§7.5 GAP-LIFE-02 and §7.2 GAP-TOOL-02** — a new sub-point. Opaque modules are not only hard
   to inspect. They are also commonly described by a single vendor claim that cannot be true at
   interface granularity.
4. **Annex A** — the model already supports this through E2 endpoint role and R3 purpose, so no
   new attribute is needed. What is needed is an **obligation**: where E4 is not software, the
   artefact MUST enumerate the relationships protecting the module itself — firmware
   authentication, administrative authentication, key backup and wrapping, and attestation — and
   not only the relationships the module serves. Add that as a conditional obligation on R1 or as
   a new conformance rule.
5. **Annex C** — add a glossary term. *Protective cryptography*: the cryptography that protects a
   component, as distinct from the cryptography the component provides.
6. **§8.2 GAP-TOOL-04 or §8.3** — one sentence. This is a case where the profile turns a single
   true-but-useless claim into six checkable ones, at no cost to the producer beyond enumeration.

### B6. Dates and owners on the recommendations

## D. Publication readiness

This is the largest single body of work remaining and it is not analytical.

**D1. The draft still says it is a draft, 60+ times.** Every chapter carries
`**Owner:** _tbd_ · **Status:** first draft — prose, not agreed text`. There are 18 `_tbd_`
instances. Page-cap instructions to the author are in the text (`**Cap:** 6pp (DR-05)`,
`**Target:** under 3pp`). Chapter 3 has a `> **Drafting note.**` blockquote and a *Resolved
drafting questions* section addressed to the drafting team. Six `> **Verify:**` markers survive
against a convention saying none should. The Editorial conventions block at the top is an
instruction sheet to authors. All of this is correct for a working draft and none of it can ship.

Recommend a **publication pass** as a distinct, scripted step rather than a hand edit: strip
status lines, caps, drafting notes, resolved-questions sections and verify markers; move the
editorial conventions into a short "How to read this report" note or delete; and have
`check-coverage.sh` gain a rule that fails if any of those patterns remain. That way the
distinction between the working repository and the submitted document is enforced rather than
remembered.

**D2. Internal tag scaffolding needs a decision.** `LYR-*`, `GAP-*`, `RDY-*` and `LSN-*` carry the
argument and the coverage check, and a policy reader can follow them — keep them, and keep
Annex C's *Report constructs* section that explains them. `DR-*`, `VL §*` and `P-*` are different:
they point at documents the reader does not have. There are dozens of them, several in
load-bearing sentences ("**Resolved (DR-09)**", "(VL §3b)"). Recommend converting `VL` references
to real citations (the verification log's purpose is served once the citation is in Annex D),
moving `DR-*` to a short annex or footnotes, and dropping `P-*` to a single sentence in §9.5.
As it stands the report cites its own filing system to a reader who cannot resolve it, which reads
as unfinished rather than rigorous.

**D3. Annex B is an empty stub, and RDY-02 depends on it.** The report recommends that the
baseline be "defined independently of serialisation and **mapped to both major formats**, with
unmappable attributes documented and raised with the specification bodies", and cites the mapping
record as the evidence for GAP-STD-01. Annex B is a four-cell table of `_tbd_`. This is the one
place where a reviewer can say the report does not do what it recommends. Two honest options:

- **Fill it** against CycloneDX 1.7 / ECMA-424 and the current SPDX release — 13 artefact-level,
  4 endpoint-level and 13 relationship-level attributes, so roughly 30 rows. This is a day or two
  of specification work and it converts RDY-02 from an assertion into a demonstration, and
  GAP-STD-01 from a claim into a finding. Strongly recommended.
- **Scope it explicitly** as forthcoming, with a date and an owner, and soften RDY-02's wording so
  the report does not claim a mapping it has not made.

Do not ship it as `_tbd_`.

**D4. Annex D needs resolvable identifiers.** No CELEX numbers, OJ references, RFC numbers, DOIs
or URLs. The annex's own note says every entry requires verification against the primary document.
For a Commission submission, each EU instrument needs its OJ citation (the CRA is Regulation (EU)
2024/2847, NIS2 is Directive (EU) 2022/2555, the Recommendation is (EU) 2024/1101 — all already
correct in the text, they just need OJ/CELEX references), each RFC its number, each BSI document

- **[check]** the CERT-In guidelines entry, marked "status to be confirmed".

- The Kloeg et al. reference needs a full citation given the weight §6.1 puts on it.

**D5. Front matter is missing.** No date, version, authorship, institutional attribution, or table
of contents; the body targets 40 pages and has no page numbers. For a government-facing output
these are not cosmetic. Memory of your conventions says Word or PDF for publication — the

**D6. The executive summary is dense.** Roughly 700 words in six bolded blocks, each of which is
