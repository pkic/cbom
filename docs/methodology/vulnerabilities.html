CONCEPT

Vulnerabilities

A CBOM says what cryptography a subject uses. Whether any of it is broken is a different statement, made by a different party, on a different schedule, and it does not belong in the document. What the methodology has to settle is how the two are joined — what a profile must require so that a responder holding a CBOM and an advisory can put them together, and what a conforming document must not be read as asserting about its own safety.

Three statements, three authors

Cryptographic weakness is communicated through three artifacts that are routinely confused because they travel together and sometimes share a file.

StatementAsserted byAssertsIs revised whenKeyed by
CBOM The producer of the subject This product applies these algorithms at these interfaces, with these implementing libraries The product changes, or a better observation of it is made The subject identifier, and a content revision
Advisory A third party: a maintainer, a CNA, a research team This component, in this version range, has this weakness Knowledge changes — new versions are implicated, or the analysis is corrected A vulnerability identifier and a component identifier
VEX statement The producer of the subject, or someone acting for it This product is, or is not, affected by that advisory, and why Either of the two above changes, or an investigation concludes A vulnerability identifier and a product identifier, together

The producer authors two of the three, which is why they tend to be merged. They should not be. A CBOM revision is an immutable point-in-time record of facts about a product; a VEX statement is a judgement about the intersection of that product with knowledge held elsewhere, and that judgement changes while the product sits still. Merging them means either republishing the CBOM every time a feed moves, or holding a document that looks authoritative and is out of date in the part a reader most relies on.

This is decision 0002, applied to a second kind of judgement

Policy Evaluation establishes that a CBOM records facts and that judgements derived from them are produced by external, versioned policy at the time of asking. Post-quantum posture was the case that forced it and pqcPosture was removed for it. Vulnerability status is the same kind of thing and fails in the same way: "this interface is not affected" is a conclusion drawn from facts the producer holds and knowledge it does not own, against criteria revised by other people. Stored in the document it goes stale silently, and a signed, dated CBOM asserting that a product is unaffected is worse than one that says nothing, because it will be believed.

So the methodology's position is the one decision 0002 already implies, stated explicitly for this case: a profile neither requires nor permits a vulnerability verdict as an attribute. It requires the facts a verdict is computed from. Recorded as decision 0020.

Two kinds of cryptographic weakness

The advice above is easy to follow for one kind of weakness and nearly vacuous for the other, and the methodology is only useful if it says which is which.

An implementation defect is a bug in code that does cryptography: a buffer overrun in a TLS extension parser, a timing side channel in an RSA decryption path, a padding oracle. It has a version range, a patch, and an identifier in a feed. It is discovered on a date and did not exist as a known weakness the day before, although the code was the same.

An algorithm or parameter weakness is a property of a choice rather than a defect in code: SHA-1 used for signatures, RSA at 1024 bits, a static Diffie-Hellman group, or any classical key exchange considered against an adversary with a quantum computer. The implementation may be flawless. There is no patch, because nothing is broken in the sense a patch fixes; the remedy is to use something else, which is a change to a configuration, a protocol negotiation, or a product.

Implementation defectAlgorithm or parameter weakness
Identified byA CVE or equivalent, against a component version rangeUsually nothing. Guidance deprecates an algorithm; no identifier is issued against a product for using it
Communicated byAn advisory, then a VEX statement per productGuidance documents and national profiles, addressed to the industry rather than to a product
RemedyUpdate the componentChange the algorithm, which may require configuration, software or hardware
Found byMatching the implementing library against a feedEvaluating disclosed algorithms against current guidance — a policy evaluation
What the CBOM doesNarrows the blast radius: which interfaces use the implicated library, and what each one is forIs the only evidence there is

The asymmetry in the last row decides where effort goes. For implementation defects the CBOM is an amplifier of machinery that already exists and works; the feeds, the identifiers and the VEX formats are all in place, and a CBOM makes their output more precise. For algorithm weakness there is no machinery: no feed emits "this product signs with SHA-1", no patch arrives, and a scanner that does not know which interface uses which algorithm cannot tell an operator what to change. That case is the reason a CBOM profile exists, and it is served by the disclosure rules plus an external policy, not by anything vulnerability-shaped.

The boundary is not perfectly clean and pretending otherwise would mislead. Protocol-level weaknesses that are exploitable through a specific implementation's downgrade behaviour do get identifiers — the attacks on export-grade ciphersuites and on weak Diffie-Hellman groups were issued CVEs, because there was an implementation path to name. What has no identifier is the general, uncontested case: current, correct, unpatched code using an algorithm that guidance now says to stop using.

What the CBOM contributes that an SBOM does not

An advisory lands against a cryptographic library. An SBOM answers one question: is that library in this product, at a version in range. That is enough to know whether to care and not enough to decide what to do.

A CBOM conforming to the interface disclosure baseline answers the next three, because its rules are written about interfaces rather than about components. Rule I9 gives the implementing library per interface, I7 gives the kind of interface, I6 gives the endpoint roles and I1 and I2 give the protocol and version. So a responder learns which interfaces use the implicated library, whether the management interface is among them, and whether the product is the initiating or the accepting party at each. The difference between "openssl 3.0.7 is present somewhere" and "three interfaces use it, one of them is the administrative login, and the product accepts connections on all three" is the difference between an advisory that has to be triaged and one that can be acted on.

Product rule P2 carries unusual weight here for a reason that has nothing to do with cryptography: the management interface is the one a responder most needs to find and the one most often left out of a disclosure. A profile that only constrains the interfaces a producer chose to declare cannot catch its omission, which is why the baseline requires the interface to be declared or its absence stated.

The join, and what it is made of

Nothing above works unless the two artifacts can be put together mechanically. Three things make that possible, and all three are already in the methodology for other reasons.

  • An identifier in a scheme the feeds use. I9 requires the implementing library as a Package URL, and the profile declares that scheme rather than leaving it per document, so that two disclosures about the same library correlate. A product name and a marketing version do not join to anything. The Formats mapping notes that provider identity is the one requirement SPDX carries natively today, through packageUrl, for exactly this reason.
  • A lifecycle stage, so the responder knows what was described. I8 says whether the reported state is intended, implemented, configured or observed. An incident needs the running deployment; a document describing the built artifact answers a different question and may name a library the deployment does not load. A profile for this use case should accept configured and observed and refuse the other two, which is a narrowing of scope.lifecycleStages and needs no new machinery.
  • A binding from the statement to the document revision. A conformance claim already binds a document by digest. The same device lets a VEX statement say which CBOM revision its analysis was performed against, which is what makes the analysis re-checkable after the product is patched and the CBOM reissued.

Where the join breaks

The attribute this use case depends on almost entirely is the one attribute the baseline permits a producer to withhold. I9 is a MUST with withholdable: true, and a document supplying the marker instead of a purl conforms. For procurement or for supply-chain disclosure that is a defensible bargain, and it is deliberate: the library and its version are what a supplier is most often unwilling to publish, as Confidentiality sets out, and a baseline that demanded them would have been refused rather than negotiated.

For incident response it is fatal. A withheld I9 leaves the responder with an interface it cannot match against a feed, and no amount of other disclosure substitutes. Two consequences follow and neither is comfortable.

  • A vulnerability-response profile has to remove withholdability from I9. That is a tightening, it is monotone, and the validator already enforces it — the PQC migration profile does precisely this to I9 for a migration reason.
  • Removing it makes that profile clearable only by a restricted variant, in the sense of Confidentiality. The most time-critical consumer is therefore the one least served by a public document, and the responder who most needs the data is often the one with no agreement in place — a downstream operator reading a vendor's published CBOM during an incident. Recorded as Q57.

Carrying both in one document

CycloneDX permits a BOM to carry a vulnerabilities array, and a VEX document in that format may be standalone or embedded in the same file as the inventory it refers to. So the question is not theoretical: a producer can hand over one file containing a CBOM and a set of vulnerability assertions, and a consumer can evaluate that file against a profile.

The methodology's position is that the two are joined, not merged, and the practical rule is about what a verdict means rather than about what a file may contain:

  • A profile neither requires nor forbids an embedded vulnerability array. Forbidding it would be a ceiling, and a profile sets a floor and cannot set a ceiling.
  • A profile's rules do not constrain that array, and conformance asserts nothing about it. A passing verdict on a document that also contains vulnerability assertions says the cryptographic disclosure met the profile. It does not say the assertions are true, current, complete, or assessed by anyone.
  • A consumer must not read the absence of vulnerability entries as a statement that there are none. Absence of a statement is not a statement, which is the same principle the disclosure states apply at attribute level: undeclared is not none.

There is a format asymmetry underneath this, of the kind decision 0016 dealt with for claims. CycloneDX can carry both in one file; SPDX 3.0.1 expresses vulnerability assessment through its own security relationships, and a CBOM referenced from an SPDX SBOM sits in a separate document. A statement of the form "the CBOM and the VEX are in one artifact" is therefore true in one carrier and not in the other, and a profile must not be written so that it depends on which. Recorded as Q58.

Algorithm weakness has no identifier

A responder acting on an implementation defect can cite CVE-XXXX-NNNNN in a ticket, suppress it with a justification, track it to closure, and prove later what was decided. None of that is available for "this interface signs with SHA-1". There is no identifier to cite, so the finding cannot be suppressed with a reason, deduplicated across reports, or compared between tools — and two scanners will report it in two incomparable ways.

Three things are sometimes pressed into the role and none of them fits:

  • CWE classifies the weakness type — CWE-327 for a broken or risky algorithm, CWE-326 for inadequate encryption strength, CWE-328 for a weak hash. It is a category, not an instance: every SHA-1 signature in an estate is CWE-328, which makes it useless as something to track to closure.
  • A guidance clause — a section of a national technical guideline — identifies the criterion rather than the occurrence, and changes when the guidance is revised, which is precisely the instability the methodology keeps out of documents.
  • A CVE against the product is a category error and the feeds rightly resist it. Using a deprecated algorithm in a supported configuration is not a defect in the product's code.

What the methodology has instead is the policy evaluation: a versioned, dated policy applied to disclosed facts, producing a finding that carries the policy version and the date. That is reproducible and auditable, and it is not citable the way a CVE is — a finding is identified by the policy and the facts it was applied to, not by a name in a register anyone else recognises. Whether that gap should be filled, and by whom, is Q59.

Two traces

Both start from the nginx subject used throughout this documentation.

An advisory against a library. An advisory is published against the OpenSSL version the subject's CBOM names at I9. The SBOM says the package is present. The CBOM says three interfaces use it: the HTTPS service interface, a TLS interface to an upstream application server, and the management interface. I6 says the product accepts on the first and initiates on the second, so if the defect is reachable only by a malicious server the upstream interface is the exposure and the public one is not. The producer then publishes a VEX statement: affected for the product with a remediation, or not_affected with a justification — the vulnerable code is not present, not in the execute path, not reachable by an adversary, or mitigated inline. That justification vocabulary exists for the same reason the methodology's disclosure markers do: a negative statement is only useful if it carries its reason. Nothing about this trace is written into the CBOM.

Guidance deprecates an algorithm. Guidance withdraws SHA-1 for signature generation. No advisory is published, no CVE is issued, no feed fires and no scanner reports anything, because nothing about the product changed. The CBOM's authentication and certificate signature facts are the only record of which interfaces are affected, and a policy evaluation over the estate's CBOMs is the only thing that produces a list. The remedy is a configuration change at one interface and a product replacement at another, and which it is can be read from the disclosed facts. This is the case the methodology is built for, and it is the case the vulnerability ecosystem does not address.

What a conforming CBOM does not assert

Conformance sets this out in general; the vulnerability-specific reading is worth stating because it is the most common misreading of a verdict. A document that conforms to a profile does not assert that the cryptography it describes is safe, current, unaffected by any advisory, or adequate for any purpose. It asserts that the required facts were disclosed in the required form. A profile is a reporting bar. Whether the facts disclosed are good news is a judgement, and it is made somewhere else, by someone else, against criteria that will have changed by the time anyone asks again.

What is not settled

  • Q57. Must a vulnerability-response profile remove withholdability from I9, and if it must, is the consequence acceptable — that the most urgent consumer can only be served by a restricted variant, and that a public CBOM is structurally unable to support incident response?
  • Q58. May a conforming document carry vulnerability assertions in the same file, and does a verdict say anything about them? The draft says yes and nothing respectively; the format asymmetry between CycloneDX and SPDX 3.0.1 means the answer cannot be expressed as a constraint on the file.
  • Q59. Is an identifier scheme needed for algorithm-class weakness, so that a finding derived from a CBOM can be cited, tracked and suppressed the way a CVE can?
  • Q60. When a product is patched and a new CBOM revision is published, is there an obligation to re-issue the VEX statements bound to the previous revision's digest, and who holds it?
In summary: a CBOM states cryptographic facts; an advisory states a weakness in a component; a VEX statement states whether this product is affected. The producer writes the first and the third and they must not be merged, because one changes when the product changes and the other when knowledge does. A profile requires the facts that make the join possible — a library identifier in a scheme the feeds use, a lifecycle stage saying what was described, and a digest the statement can be bound to — and requires no verdict, which is decision 0002 reaching a second kind of judgement. For implementation defects the CBOM makes existing machinery precise. For algorithm weakness there is no machinery, no identifier and no patch, and the CBOM is the only evidence there is.
