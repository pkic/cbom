OPERATIONS

Regulation

Regulators and policy makers increasingly ask for a cryptographic inventory, but most instruments do not say what the inventory must contain. This register records, clause by clause, whether a CBOM that conforms to a named profile gives a reader the facts needed to show that an instrument is met. It is a living register, kept outside the release, because instruments change more often than the methodology does. Publication 3, section 16.4, describes the method.

How an instrument is recorded

Each clause is broken into evidence questions: things a reader must be able to answer to show that the clause is met. Each question names the profile rules that supply its facts, and is tested at three levels:

  • Expressible. Can the methodology's model express the facts? If not, the gap is in the methodology and leads to a candidate attribute.
  • Required. Does a named profile require the facts? If not, a reader can still ask for them through a deeper profile.
  • Evaluable. Can a policy compute the answer without a person's judgement? If not, the policy must hold an allow list, a threshold or the operator's input.

Each question then gets one verdict:

VerdictMeaning
SufficientA CBOM that conforms to the profile named answers the question.
Deeper profileThe model has the facts, but only a deeper or more specific profile requires them.
PartialThe CBOM supplies part of the answer, and the operator supplies the rest.
JudgementThe CBOM supplies the facts, but the answer is a judgement that belongs in a versioned policy.
Out of scopeThe question is about an organisation or a process, and no CBOM can answer it.
OpenThe text that defines the question has not been published yet.

Cyber Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Annex I

EU, binding regulation, 2024-11-20. Applies to: Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market. Reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026, and the rest of the regulation from 11 December 2027.

Annex I sets essential cybersecurity requirements. Part I covers the properties of the product, including confidentiality by state-of-the-art encryption and the integrity of data, commands and configuration. Part II covers vulnerability handling, including documenting components in an SBOM that covers at least the top-level dependencies, and distributing updates securely. The cryptographic requirements of the harmonised standards are still being drafted. They include an allow list of mechanisms, key-management controls and crypto agility.

Source: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2847/oj

DORA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, and Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1774, Articles 6 and 7

EU, binding regulation, 2024-03-13. Applies to: Financial entities under DORA, other than those under the simplified ICT risk-management framework.

Article 6 requires a documented policy on encryption and cryptographic controls, covering data at rest and in transit, data in use where necessary, internal network connections and traffic with external parties, and key management. Article 6(4) requires provisions for updating or changing the cryptographic technology when developments in cryptanalysis require it. Article 7 requires key management across the whole lifecycle, and a register of all certificates and certificate-storing devices, at least for the ICT assets that support critical or important functions, kept up to date. The recitals ask financial entities to keep abreast of developments in cryptanalysis.

Source: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_del/2024/1774/oj

NIS Cooperation Group, coordinated implementation roadmap for the transition to post-quantum cryptography

EU, recommendation, 2025-06. Applies to: EU Member States, and through national roadmaps, operators of high-risk and medium-risk use cases.

Three milestones. By 31 December 2026, Member States complete the First Steps, including cryptographic asset management, dependency mapping and quantum risk analysis. By 31 December 2030, the transition is complete for high-risk use cases and cryptographic agility is adopted. By 31 December 2035, it is complete for medium-risk use cases, and for low-risk use cases where feasible. Section 6.2 names CBOM.

NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555, Article 21(2)(h), and Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2690, Annex, point 9

EU, binding regulation, 2024-10-17. Applies to: Essential and important entities under NIS2. The implementing regulation applies directly to the entity types it names, such as cloud, data centre, managed service and trust service providers.

Article 21(2)(h) requires policies and procedures on the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption. Point 9 of the Annex to the implementing regulation says what the policy must contain: the cryptographic measures for each class of asset, the protocols and algorithm families with a crypto-agility approach where appropriate, and key management. It also requires the policy to be reviewed. Neither text explicitly requires an inventory of cryptographic assets.

Source: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2024/2690/oj

NIST IR 8547, Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards

US, guidance, 2026-10-27. Applies to: US federal agencies, and suppliers that follow NIST's transition schedule.

Sets the dates after which quantum-vulnerable signature and key-establishment algorithms are deprecated (after 2030) and disallowed (after 2035), at every security strength. The 2024 draft deprecated only the weakest strengths.

Source: https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8547/final

PCI DSS v4.0.1, requirements 4.2.1.1 and 12.3.3

international, industry standard, 2024-06. Applies to: Entities that store, process or transmit cardholder data, and service providers that can affect its security. Both requirements have been assessed since 31 March 2025.

Requirement 4.2.1.1 asks for an inventory of the entity's trusted keys and certificates used to protect the primary account number during transmission. Requirement 12.3.3 asks for the cipher suites and protocols in use to be documented and reviewed at least once every 12 months: an up-to-date inventory with their purpose and where they are used, active monitoring of industry trends on their continued viability, and a documented plan to respond to anticipated changes in cryptographic vulnerabilities. It applies to every cipher suite and protocol used to meet PCI DSS requirements, including those that protect card numbers in storage and in transmission, protect passwords, and authenticate access.

Source: https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/

US, executive order and implementing memorandum, 2026-06-22. Applies to: US federal civilian agencies, and through the Federal Acquisition Regulation, covered contractors.

The order requires agencies to review the cryptographic inventories of high value assets and high impact systems, sets dates for post-quantum key establishment (end of 2030) and signatures (end of 2031) on those systems, and asks CISA and NIST to publish minimum elements for a CBOM within 270 days. The memorandum, of 24 June 2026, states that inventory data should populate a central CBOM, without naming a format.

NSA Commercial National Security Algorithm Suite 2.0 (CNSA 2.0)

US, guidance for national security systems, 2022-09. Applies to: Owners, operators and vendors of US national security systems.

Names the algorithms for national security systems. The current FAQ (version 2.1, December 2024) gives the dates of CNSSP 15: new acquisitions must be CNSA 2.0 compliant from 1 January 2027, equipment and services that cannot support CNSA 2.0 are phased out by 31 December 2030, and CNSA 2.0 is mandated by 31 December 2031, each unless otherwise noted. For firmware and software signing it prefers LMS or XMSS now. The earlier FAQ (version 2.0, April 2024) set two dates for each category of technology, for example software and firmware signing (prefer by 2025, exclusively by 2030) and operating systems (2027 and 2033). Version 2.1 does not repeat that table.

Source: https://media.defense.gov/2022/Sep/07/2003071836/-1/-1/0/CSI_CNSA_2.0_FAQ_.PDF
