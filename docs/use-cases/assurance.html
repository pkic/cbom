GROUP

Assurance: a reader relies on the document as evidence

Use cases in which the reader is an auditor, an assessor or a regulator who did not produce the document, cannot ask follow-up questions, and has to rely on it as evidence. The cryptographic facts are usually the disclosure facts. What changes is what the document has to show about itself: who made it, when, from what, whether it has been altered since, and whether it is still current.

Proposed in October 2026 as an amendment to draft decision 0023, which defined the first two groups. Not yet settled.
This is the least settled of the four groups. The working group may find that its requirements belong on the conformance claim, which any profile can carry, rather than in profiles of their own. The first worked example will show which.

What the group shares

Provenance and freshness carry the weight. A reported position is only meaningful to an external reader if it says when and from what it was derived, which makes the lifecycle stage and the date of each statement as important as the statement. Beyond that, an assurance reader needs to know that the document it holds is the one that was evaluated, and that nothing has replaced it.

The methodology already provides part of this. A conformance claim is bound to one document by digest and lists each profile separately (decision 0015), and a claim can be re-checked. What it does not yet say is how the digest is computed so that two producers agree, whether profiles themselves are signed, who may declare conformance, and what a claim means once the document it binds is superseded.

One document can serve both disclosure and assurance. An assurance profile adds requirements on the document's provenance and on its claim, not new cryptographic facts.

Worked examples

None yet. A first one could take the Keycloak supplier document, add a conformance claim bound by digest, and work through what a regulator receiving it under NIS2 would still need to know before relying on it.

The use cases in this group

Regulatory and compliance reporting

An organization must demonstrate its cryptographic position against a framework such as the EU Cyber Resilience Act, NIS2, the BSI technical guidelines, or a national directive. The consumer is an auditor or regulator who did not generate the data and cannot ask follow-up questions of the system.

The cryptographic facts are largely the disclosure baseline's. A profile encodes what a given framework expects, so that conformance can be evidenced by a dated artifact that an auditor can re-check. Because the reader is external, provenance and lifecycle stage carry unusual weight: a reported position is only meaningful if it says when and from what it was derived. A jurisdiction-specific profile also lets a global vendor satisfy the requirement once and report against it in several places.

Certification and validation evidence

An evaluator or certification body checks a product against a scheme such as FIPS 140-3 module validation, Common Criteria or IEC 62443-4-2, and decides whether the cryptography a CBOM describes matches what was evaluated. A buyer asks the same question when it relies on a supplier's claim of validation. The cryptographic facts already exist: the module validation attributes of the PQC migration profile, and the certifications field of CycloneDX 2.0. The evidence question is whether the document is bound to the evaluated version and configuration, and whether it still is.

Proposed.

Readiness assessment evidence

An assessor scores an organization against a readiness or maturity model, or against a national migration roadmap, and decides what level or status to record. A conforming CBOM could serve as evidence for some of the criteria, provided the assessor can tell when and from what it was produced. Whether to propose CBOMs as evidence to the owners of such models is Q44, and is an outreach decision as much as a methodology one.

Proposed.
